Welcome in

Before any camera, light or tool, every film here starts the same way: with a person who wants something, and a feeling you can already picture. Hold these five ideas loosely and come back to them whenever a story feels stuck.

  1. Lean on the character, not the theme or the plot.
  2. Hold the want. Write one line for what one person in the story wants more than anything, like “she wants to be left alone” or “he wants his brother back.”
  3. The emotion, the conflict and even the theme grow out of that one sentence.
  4. Character, feeling and theme lean on each other in a loop. None comes first in a fixed order: start wherever you have the most and let the rest surface.
  5. Collect before you create. Before writing, gather five examples of the feeling you want, from films or scenes you love, and note in one line what each one does to you. That gives you data points instead of guessing.

The Experience Machine

The hook

A machine can give you a perfect life forever, but none of it would be real. Most people say no. Here’s why that’s strange.

The first ten seconds stitch two scenes together: the Utah road as the opening emotional image (wide exterior) and the kitchen as her interior (close contrast). The arc: isolation opening outward, then turning inward.
