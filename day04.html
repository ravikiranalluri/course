Section 2

Reading

Video (watch) · Exercise (hands-on)

Composition is where you ask the eye to go first. Five tools cover most of it:

  • Rule of thirds: divide the frame 3 by 3; put eyes or horizons on the lines. Centered framing is a choice too: it feels formal, iconic, sometimes trapped.
  • Leading lines: roads, table edges, light beams pull the eye toward the subject.
  • Negative space: empty area around a person. Lots of it equals loneliness.
  • Headroom and look room: leave space in the direction a person looks or walks.
  • The golden triangle: diagonal shapes that cradle the subject. In your road shot, the road's two edges converge into a triangle with her at the point.
Worked example

Your road frame, composed

From your Oct 24 answers: "She sits in a golden triangle at the center of the frame." So:

  • Horizon on the lower third line (lots of sky = open, exposed).
  • The road's edges are leading lines converging at the center, forming the triangle.
  • She is centered, on purpose: she's walking straight into us, so centered feels direct and confrontational.
  • Huge negative space of sky on both sides = isolation.

Every rule here serves the feeling. Break one only when you can say what breaking it means.

Section 3

Exercise (hands-on)

Video (watch) · Reading

  1. Find 5 frames you love (any films). Draw thirds lines over each (on paper or a phone markup).
  2. For each, write which tool is doing the work (thirds, lines, negative space, triangle, centered).
  3. Sketch the road frame twice: once with the horizon low, once high. Write which feels more isolated and why.
  4. Sketch the kitchen frame with the keys placed so the eye finds them second, after her hands.
What good looks like
  • Each of your 5 frames has one named tool, not 'it's balanced'.
  • You can say why the low horizon (big sky) reads lonelier than the high one.
  • In the kitchen sketch, the keys are visible but not centered: found second, not first.
