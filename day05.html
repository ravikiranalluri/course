Section 2

Reading

Video (watch) · Exercise (hands-on)

A cut is a sentence break. The viewer's brain joins two shots into one meaning: a face, then keys, means she's thinking about the keys. That's the Kuleshov effect, and it means a lot of your story lives between shots.

Walter Murch ranks what a cut should respect, in order: emotion (by far the most), story, rhythm, eye-trace, the 2D plane of the screen, and 3D space. If the feeling is right, viewers forgive almost everything else.

Pacing for your channel: a visual change every 3 to 8 seconds keeps attention, unless you are holding on purpose. A held shot after fast cuts is the loudest thing an editor can do.

Read more: The Rule of Six (summary).

Worked example

The kitchen's rhythm

Kitchen, seconds 8-10: fast knife rhythm, cut, cut, cut (hands, knife, hands), then the near-miss, then one held shot with near-silence and the keys in frame. The held shot is where the reflection lives. If you cut away too soon, the pause dies.

Kuleshov at work: her still face, then the keys. We never say she's undecided; the cut says it.

Section 3

Exercise (hands-on)

Video (watch) · Reading

  1. Take any 30 seconds of a film you like. Count the cuts and time each shot with a stopwatch.
  2. Write the shot lengths as a row of numbers (for example 3, 2, 2, 6, 1, 8). Mark where the longest hold is and what it's for.
  3. On paper, write the shot lengths you want for the 10-second opening (road, face, kitchen). Mark your one long hold.
  4. Kuleshov test: photograph your own neutral face, then put it next to three different photos (keys, a road, a knife). Write what each pair seems to mean.
What good looks like
  • A row of real numbers, and you can explain the longest hold.
  • Your opening plan has variety (not all equal lengths) and one deliberate hold.
  • Your Kuleshov pairs read as three different thoughts from the same face.
